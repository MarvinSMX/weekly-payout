📚 Weekly Dividend ETF Education

The metrics that actually matter when you compare weekly payers

📈 What is Total Return?

Total return combines every dividend the fund has distributed with the change in its share price since inception. A fund can pay a huge yield and still have a weak total return if the share price fell just as fast. This is the number that tells you whether you actually came out ahead.

💵 What is Dividend Yield?

Dividend yield is the annualized distribution divided by the share price, shown as a percentage. It tells you the size of the payment relative to what you invested. It does not tell you where that payment came from, or whether it is sustainable.

📉 What is Price Decay?

Price decay means the share price sits below where it started at inception. It is common in option income and leveraged weekly payers, where big distributions are partly funded by the fund's own capital. Price decay references share price only and is never a buy or sell signal.

🗓️ Why Weekly Payments?

Weekly payers distribute roughly 52 times a year instead of 4 or 12. That cadence lets you reinvest faster, which compounds quicker, and it smooths cash flow if you are living off distributions. Payment size usually varies week to week.

⚠️ Why Yield Alone Misleads

A 90% yield on a fund that lost 60% of its share price is not a 90% return. Pair every yield figure with total return and the price decay flag before drawing a conclusion. The two tables on this page are built to be read together for exactly that reason.

🏦 Who Issues WeeklyPayout?

Most weekly payers come from YieldMax, Roundhill, Defiance and GraniteShares. Each uses a different mechanic, from synthetic covered calls to leveraged single-stock exposure, and those differences drive most of the gap in total returns you see above.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is total return on a weekly dividend ETF?

Total return combines every dividend the fund has distributed with the change in its share price since inception. Because many weekly payers lose share price over time, total return is the only number that shows whether the income actually left you ahead.

Why does a high yield weekly ETF sometimes have a low total return?

If the share price falls faster than the distributions accumulate, the headline yield stays high while the total return stays flat or negative. A yield figure on its own tells you the size of the payment, not whether you gained anything. That is why this page ranks by both.

What does "No Price Decay" actually mean?

It means the ETF's share price is at or above where it started at inception. The fund has paid out distributions without eroding the principal behind them. This is uncommon among high yield weekly payers, which is exactly why the second table above is worth attention.

How often do weekly dividend ETFs pay?

Weekly payers distribute roughly 52 times per year, usually on a fixed weekday. That cadence allows faster reinvestment and compounding than monthly or quarterly schedules, though the payment amount typically varies week to week based on option premiums or fund performance.

Should I pick the highest yield or the highest total return?

It depends on what you need. If you are drawing income right now and can tolerate a declining share price, yield matters most. If you are building a position over years, total return matters more. Many investors blend both, which is why both tables are on this page.

How often is this data updated?

The rankings pull live from the WeeklyPayout.com master database and refresh daily. Market data moves quickly, so figures here can lag the market. Always verify with the fund issuer before acting on anything you read.

Are weekly dividend ETFs riskier than traditional dividend ETFs?

Generally yes. Most weekly payers use options or leverage on concentrated single-stock or index exposure, which introduces mechanics traditional dividend funds do not have. Higher distribution rates usually come with higher volatility and greater potential for share price erosion.

Where does this data come from?

All figures are gathered from publicly available fund and market data and compiled into the WeeklyPayout.com database. Data can be inaccurate, delayed or outdated. Nothing on this page is a recommendation to buy or sell any security.
